abstract: ", \xC9lie Bouh\xE9reau, the first librarian of Marsh\u2019s Library in\
  \ Dublin had, as the editors of this volume point out, \u2018the misfortune to live\
  \ in interesting times\u2019. As a Huguenot in La Rochelle his life grew increasing\
  \ arduous as Louis XIV imposed growing restrictions on the rights of Protestants.\
  \ In 1685, following the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, Bouh\xE9reau sent his\
  \ books and papers to England, together with the archives of the Reformed Church\
  \ of La Rochelle, and, a few months later, he followed them, along with most of\
  \ his family. There followed a decade of travelling, during which he served in various\
  \ roles and at the end of which he was offered the post of librarian of the new\
  \ library which Narcissus Marsh, archbishop of Dublin, was in the process of establishing.,\
  \ Bouh\xE9reau\u2019s biography has been documented in a number of publications,\
  \ but this volume places his life against the background of Huguenot history and\
  \ examines his books and papers in more detail than hitherto. It is the result of\
  \ a conference held at Marsh\u2019s Library in November 2019., The book is divided\
  \ into four parts. In the first section, Jean-Paul Pittion provides a biography\
  \ of Bouh\xE9reau, and Muriel Hoareau discusses his background as a Protestant in\
  \ seventeenth-century La Rochelle. The second part, \u2018Community Creations\u2019\
  , examines his network of contacts, including his encounters with John Locke and\
  \ with the lawyer Abraham Tessereau who assembled \u2018two sprawling [End Page\
  \ 127] volumes\u2019 filled with notes and printed publications documenting the\
  \ persecution of the Huguenots. The chapter by David van der Linden is a detailed\
  \ examination of these volumes, which have been largely neglected by historians\
  \ and which, thanks to the friendship of Tessereau and Bouh\xE9reau, are now among\
  \ the collections at Marsh\u2019s. Aspects of Huguenot religious life are the subject\
  \ of two chapters, one by Jane McKee on psalms and sonnets, which form the subject\
  \ of correspondence between Boh\xE9reau and the clergyman Laurent Drelincourt, and\
  \ the second, by Eleanor Jones-McAuley, on Huguenot worship music in eighteenth-century\
  \ Dublin., The first chapter of the third section, by Amy Prendergast, uses Bouh\xE9\
  reau\u2019s diary to provide an insight into diplomatic life in the late seventeenth\
  \ century as seen through the eyes of the writer, who served as secretary to Thomas\
  \ Coxe, William III\u2019s envoy to the Swiss cantons. The significant role played\
  \ by women in the development of diplomatic networks is especially revealing. Bouh\xE9\
  reau\u2019s diary and account book are used by Charles Ivar McGrath to consider\
  \ the day-to-day management of money in Ireland at the time, including the frustrations\
  \ of an unstable exchange rate between sterling and the Irish pound. In the final\
  \ chapter in this section, Marie L\xE9outre also considers eighteenth-century accountancy\
  \ and financial transactions, using as evidence the relationship between Bouh\xE9\
  reau and another Huguenot, Henri de Massue de Ruvigny, later earl of Galway, and\
  \ whom Bouh\xE9reau served as secretary from 1693., The final section will probably\
  \ be of most interest to readers of The Library. Entitled \u2018Loss, Return, and\
  \ Reconstruction\u2019, it considers what has been \u2018lost, dispersed, or repatriated\u2019\
  \ from Bouh\xE9reau\u2019s papers and books. Bouh\xE9reau left a notebook containing\
  \ over 200 pages of notes, diary entries, and accounts, which Noreen Humble examines\
  \ in detail. It is clear that the surviving notebook is just the third of three,\
  \ the first two of which seem to have contained notes on his reading, as Humble\
  \ has been able to glean from the cross-references still extant in Bouh\xE9reau\u2019\
