abstract: This article reviews the historiography and statistical basis of Irish woodland
  cover between the mid-seventeenth century and the 1920s. It argues that the estimates
  of woodland cover generated by Oliver Rackham from the Civil Survey returns in the
  1650s are far too low, while the figures presented by Eileen McCracken for the late
  nineteenth and early twentieth centuries are incorrectly presented, leaving a confused
  overall picture of Irish woodland cover. Firstly, a new estimate is generated for
  the 1650s and this can be compared to the official statistics for woodlands and
  plantations published between the mid-nineteenth century and 1917, providing a more
  accurate picture of the decline of Irish woodland in the intervening years. Secondly,
  a more detailed assessment is made of the official felling statistics published
  annually between 1890 and 1917, in order to provide greater insight into the nature
  and extent of the Irish forestry sector in this period. The article concludes that
  Irish woodland cover declined dramatically from around 6.9% in the mid-seventeenth
  century to about 1.5% in the mid-nineteenth century, leaving an exceptionally low
  level of woodland cover, which declined even more between 1880 and 1920, when Irish
  woodland cover reached an all time historical low point. The article concludes that
  Irish forestry like most British forestry was small scale, poorly managed and unprofitable
  by European standards.
