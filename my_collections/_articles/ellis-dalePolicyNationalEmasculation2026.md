abstract: This article argues that, during the Second World War, Irish neutrality
  was constantly debated in gendered terms. For its proponents, neutrality was situated
  within a nationalist tradition which spoke a markedly masculine language and thus
  supplied a useful justification for a diplomatic and military policy which was unavoidable
  for the Irish state. At the same time, the numerous critics of Irish neutrality
  routinely drew upon a gendered language of emasculation and cowardice. For both
  sides, the masculinity of the Irishman in Allied service offered a useful rhetorical
  device in arguments both for and against active participation in the Second World
  War. In doing so, this article thus highlights the complex relationship between
  masculinity and violence in Irish history. Anxieties around masculinity and the
  gendered order motivated and supplied rhetorical ballast to the policy of neutrality
  and thus signified a subtle shift in Irish hegemonic masculinity during this time.
  Whereas previously, Irish masculinity had been predicated on physical force violence
  and heroism, it increasingly emphasised rational self-restraint, comradeship in
  a communal effort and participation in a patriarchal, agrarian social order.
