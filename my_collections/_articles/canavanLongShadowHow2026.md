abstract: "Why do some publics engage deeply with distant conflicts absent direct\
  \ ethnic or strategic ties? Existing explanations emphasise geopolitical ties, demographics,\
  \ or elite cues, but these cannot account for the Republic of Ireland\u2019s consistent\
  \ pro-Palestinian stance, exceptional in Western Europe and central in shaping Irish\
  \ state behaviour, international coalitions, and bilateral relations. We argue that\
  \ historical narratives \u2013 particularly those rooted in anticolonial struggle\
  \ \u2013 provide cognitive and affective mechanisms that shape public attitudes\
  \ to foreign conflict. Using novel survey measures and open questions on a representative\
  \ sample of Irish respondents (1547), we find that sympathy for Palestinians is\
  \ broad-based, deeply held, and closely tied to perceptions of Irish historical\
  \ oppression and unity. These results hold after accounting for ideology and party\
  \ cues. Given its prominence in public debate, we also test antisemitism as an explanation.\
  \ We find low levels of antisemitism and higher levels of Islamophobia, indicating\
  \ that general pro-Palestinian sympathy is driven neither by prejudice toward Jews\
  \ nor particular affinity toward Muslims. Open-ended responses show Irish respondents\
  \ spontaneously draw analogies between Ireland\u2019s colonial past and the Palestinian\
  \ experience. Our findings highlight the long shadow of national history in shaping\
  \ contemporary foreign policy attitudes."
