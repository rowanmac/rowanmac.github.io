abstract: "The introduction of foundational employment equality legislation in the\
  \ UK and Republic of Ireland in the 1970s did not automatically create a more equitable\
  \ workplace. While legislation such as the Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 and\
  \ Employment Equality Act 1977 in the Republic of Ireland, and the Equal Pay Act\
  \ 1970 and Sex Discrimination Act 1975 in the United Kingdom, addressed long-established\
  \ workplace inequities such as gendered pay scales and sex discrimination, many\
  \ employers chose to evade the new laws. Women workers were often forced to pursue\
  \ employment equality cases to avail of their rights\u2014a decision with the potential\
  \ to jeopardise an established livelihood. This article explores three contrasting\
  \ cases and their wider context; a 1976 British sex discrimination taken by Vivien\
  \ Saunders, a golf professional, a 1978 case pursued by Senator Mary Robinson in\
  \ the ROI and a 1979 equal pay claim made by Glen Electric\u2019s employees in Northern\
  \ Ireland. These women\u2019s actions expanded definitions of activism and served\
  \ to test the new law\u2019s provisions, creating a fairer workplace for future\
  \ generations of women workers."
