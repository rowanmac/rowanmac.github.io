abstract: "Recent historical research has emphasised that Irish nationalism and the\
  \ Irish Free State were neither as backward-looking nor as fixated on the Anglo-Irish\
  \ relationship as previously thought. Instead, new work has shown that the revolutionary\
  \ generation was deeply engaged with the world beyond the United Kingdom and desired\
  \ something more than a return to an imagined nineteenth-century or even precolonial\
  \ past. Much of this work has focused on areas in which Irish nationalists believed\
  \ they could play a leading role. Sinn F\xE9in, for example, referred to Ireland\
  \ as \u2018the first of the small nations\u2019 and tried to position itself as\
  \ an avatar of decolonisation and a tutor for other, often non-white, British colonies.\
  \ However, one of the most common comparisons during this period was to Denmark,\
  \ which was not only Irish agriculture\u2019s greatest rival for the British market,\
  \ but was also depicted to Irish farmers as an exemplar of agrarian modernity and\
  \ scientific methods. This article analyses how Irish nationalists increasingly\
  \ depicted the Free State as the not-Denmark, using Danish superiority in production,\
  \ marketing, co-operatism and quality of life as a way to criticise \u2018traditional\u2019\
  \ Irish agricultural practices and urge significant reform."
