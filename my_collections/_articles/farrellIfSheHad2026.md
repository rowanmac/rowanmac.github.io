abstract: Focusing on a sample of seventy-eight abortion or attempted abortion cases
  from across nineteenth-century Ireland, this article demonstrates the importance
  of money, time, knowledge and access during a period when abortion was illegal.
  Qualitative evidence points to a variety of methods to induce a miscarriage, including
  the ingestion of drugs or other substances, the use of instruments, herbal remedies,
  bloodletting, and other non-medical techniques. The advertisement of British-based
  commercial abortifacients in Irish newspapers, and abortion tourists from Britain
  in the records, point to shared experiences of reproductive control. But both in
  cases where women wanted an abortion and those where they did not, there is some
  evidence of agency and autonomy. As demonstrated in this article, accomplices were
  also frequently involved in abortion cases, particularly the men connected to the
  pregnancies, although it must be acknowledged that the woman who acted alone is
  likely underrepresented in the historical records on abortion. The individual stories
  shed much light on abortion in nineteenth-century Ireland but are also revealing
  of lived experiences beyond, including courtship and marriage practices, reproductive
  knowledge, romantic, familial and employer/employee relationships, and networks
  of information.
