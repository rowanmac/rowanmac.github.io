abstract: "This historiographical review maps and scrutinizes the interpretative traditions\
  \ that have grown around Edmund Burke\u2019s thought since the 1790s, identifying\
  \ three distinctive strands of criticism: the Victorian utilitarian/proto-liberal\
  \ interpretation, the Namierite analysis of Burke, and the North American natural\
  \ law tradition. It then turns to the recent archival and contextual scholarship\
  \ that has largely superseded these schemata \u2013 the biographies of F. P. Lock\
  \ and Richard Bourke, the analysis of J. G. A. Pocock, and the imperial and global\
  \ perspectives of P. J. Marshall and others \u2013 which relocates Burke within\
  \ the political crises and colonial contexts in which he wrote. This reorientation\
  \ has been underpinned by the modern editorial projects behind his Correspondence\
  \ and Writings and speeches. Rather than adjudicating who Burke was, the review\
  \ traces how successive generations have read, reframed, and instrumentalized his\
  \ prima facie contradictions, suggesting that his resistance to classification reflects\
  \ less a defect in the literature than the enduring richness of its subject."
