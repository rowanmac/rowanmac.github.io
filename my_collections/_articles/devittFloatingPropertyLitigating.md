abstract: "This article examines a case brought before the Belfast Spring Assizes\
  \ in 1886 and again in 1887, in which three coastal smallholders from Ballycastle,\
  \ County Antrim, were prosecuted for theft of seaweed from shores claimed by a local\
  \ landlord. Drawing upon a variety of documents produced from the trial, this article\
  \ examines how Irish legal institutions managed the indeterminate foreshore environment\
  \ during a period of considerable land law reform. Central to this investigation\
  \ is the consideration of how evidentiary hierarchies, upheld in court proceedings\
  \ and enshrined in public judgements, informed public challenges to coastal enclosure.\
  \ Written evidence bases (maps, leases, rent books) were privileged over oral testimony,\
  \ with the effect of reinforcing absolutist, totalising visions of property ownership.\
  \ Yet, through the hearing of oral testimonies, the courtroom retained a crucial\
  \ function as a theatre for airing popular counterclaims. These depositions complicated\
  \ the prosecution\u2019s vision of coastal ownership, articulating the foreshore\
  \ as a terraqueous environment governed by local custom and informal systems of\
  \ exchange that were underpinned by understandings of reciprocity and common rights\
  \ rather than exclusive title."
